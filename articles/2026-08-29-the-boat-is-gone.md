# The Boat Is Gone

A colleague told me recently that he didn't believe email would ever be a thing. He wasn't being nostalgic. He was making a point about how badly people, including smart, senior people, misjudge which changes are actually coming.

[YOUR DETAIL HERE: confirm the decade, the firm's office, and whether this was your colleague's own project or one he heard about, so the anecdote is exact before this goes out]

Before email, if his firm needed to get something urgent to Iran, here's what actually happened. The project director wrote the letter by hand. Someone typed it up. It flew out, three days in the air before it landed. It reached the firm's office in Iran, then got carried down to the shore. A person with a boat picked it up. The answer came back spoken, not written, and someone on the other end wrote it down.

Six people. Three days at best, more if the flight or the boat ran late. All of it to move one sentence from a desk to a shore and back.

Email didn't make that faster so much as it deleted five of those six jobs in a single move: the typist, the courier, the office relay, the boat, the person who wrote down what was spoken. Nobody got called into an office and let go over it. The jobs just stopped being jobs, the way a role disappears once the reason for it is gone.

I keep coming back to that story because the same collapse is happening again, one layer further in. This time it's the person at the firm whose job used to be drafting the first version of something, so someone more senior could redline it, so someone senior to them could sign it, before a client ever saw it. AI is cutting layers out of that chain too. The difference is that the people losing a layer this time aren't couriers in another country. They're colleagues in the next office, watching it happen to their own desk.

The honest economic answer to what happens to them next isn't simple, and I'd rather give it to you straight than pick the version that sounds better. David Autor and Neil Thompson at MIT have studied exactly this question: what happens to the people still doing a job once the automated part of it is gone. The answer depends entirely on which part gets automated. When computers took over the routine side of bookkeeping between 1980 and 2018, employment in that role fell by a third, but real wages for the bookkeepers who remained climbed nearly 40 percent, because what was left required real judgment ([MIT Sloan, on Autor and Thompson's research](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/a-new-look-how-automation-changes-value-labor)). Taxi drivers had the opposite experience. GPS automated the one thing that used to separate a good driver from a new one, deep knowledge of the streets, and the job got more crowded and paid less as a result. Same word, automation, two opposite outcomes, and the difference is entirely about whether the machine ate the routine work or the differentiator.

So which side of that line is engineering and consulting on right now? The best evidence I've seen this month points to the bookkeeper side, and I hold that with real but not full confidence. A field study of 713,564 prompts from nearly 4,000 employees at one large firm, across 15 functional areas over eight months in 2025, found that sophisticated use tracked closely with seniority, and that formal AI training produced no lasting gain for anyone who went through it ([arXiv, Sophistication in GenAI Use: Field Evidence from a Large Firm](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27364)). If AI had already eaten the differentiator, that gap would be closing. The data says it's holding, at least for now. Judgment is still the scarce thing.

That's the benefit case, and it's real. It isn't the whole picture. Harvard Business Review reported in September 2025 that McKinsey's own internal AI assistant, Lilli, used by more than 70 percent of the firm's staff, cuts research time by roughly 30 percent, and that non-senior consulting job postings in Canada are down 40 percent since early 2022 ([HBR, Duncan, Anderson, and Saviano, AI Is Changing the Structure of Consulting Firms](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-is-changing-the-structure-of-consulting-firms)). Firms are restructuring around what the piece calls an obelisk shape: a narrower base, and a handful of new roles, AI facilitators, engagement architects, client leaders, built around directing the work rather than doing the base analysis AI now handles. The bookkeeper story only holds up if there are still bookkeepers being trained ten years from now to become the senior people whose judgment stays scarce. Narrow that base far enough and the supply of future seniors narrows with it. The real risk here isn't that AI takes someone's job this quarter. It's that it quietly closes the path that used to produce the next round of people whose judgment is worth paying for.

Which gets to the part your question was actually about: the sense of purpose. Gallup's newest global workplace survey, out this year, put engagement at 20 percent worldwide in 2025, the lowest since 2020, after the first two consecutive annual drops the survey has ever recorded. Eighteen percent of U.S. employees think it's likely their job gets eliminated by AI or automation within five years, rising to 23 percent inside organizations that have already adopted AI, and 32 percent in finance. Only 12 percent of employees strongly agree that AI has actually transformed how work gets done where they sit ([Gallup, State of the Global Workplace 2026](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx)).

Here's the number in that report I keep coming back to. Employees whose manager actively supports the team's use of AI are 8.7 times as likely to say AI has transformed how work gets done, and 7.4 times as likely to say it gives them more room to do what they're actually good at. Fewer than one in three employees say their manager does that. Manager engagement itself has fallen nine points since 2022, the sharpest drop of any group Gallup tracks (same source).

Put those two findings together and the purpose question mostly answers itself. A person doesn't lose their sense of usefulness the day a model drafts something they used to draft. They lose it in the gap that follows, the weeks or months where nobody with the authority to tell them what their job is now has had the time to do it. That gap is where the shock your colleague described actually lives, and it shows up as a management capacity problem before it shows up as an AI problem.

McKinsey's State of Organizations 2026 survey found fear of job loss cited by 46 percent of respondents as a top obstacle to scaling AI, ahead even of regulatory concern ([McKinsey, The State of Organizations 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/hr-monitor)). That isn't a soft HR number. It's the thing directly slowing the rollout the firm is already paying for. What Gallup's data points to as the fix isn't a town hall or a values deck. It's giving the people whose actual job is to sit with one person and redraw what they're for enough time to do that. Research on job crafting going back to Amy Wrzesniewski's work has always found employees can partly do this reframing themselves, through the tasks they take on, the relationships they build around them, and how they narrate their own role, but they do it far better with a manager who has the room to help ([Berg, Dutton, and Wrzesniewski, Job Crafting and Meaningful Work](https://www.inspiringspace.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Berg-Dutton-Wrzesniewski-2013-Job-crafting-and-meaningful-work.pdf)).

A firm's AI purpose problem is a manager bandwidth problem first, and a technology problem second.

I'll put a date on this one. By the time Gallup publishes its next State of the Global Workplace report, in 2027, manager engagement will still be falling faster than overall employee engagement, because firms are buying AI seats for individual contributors faster than they're giving managers the time to do the one thing that actually protects morale: telling each person, specifically, what they're for now. Confidence: medium.

What I'm watching next is simpler than the prediction. Whether any firm moving to the obelisk model publishes what happens to its junior cohort two or three years out, beyond the org chart it announces on the day it changes. And whether any firm starts measuring manager time spent on redefining roles after a task gets automated, the way it already measures utilization. Nobody is publishing that number yet. The firm that starts will be the one that actually understood what your colleague was describing.
