## LinkedIn Post A, publish day

In 2018 I heard artificial intelligence discussed at a conference in Slovenia and assumed it
was decades from mattering. Four years later the whole world had ChatGPT.

I had the same feeling recently listening to a podcast on quantum computing and its risk to
cryptography. This time I did not wait to find out if I was wrong again.

Quantum computers are nowhere close to designing a bridge better than a laptop can today. But
Google has already set 2029 as its own deadline for protecting its systems against a quantum
computer capable of breaking current encryption, built on research that keeps lowering the bar
for how soon that machine could exist.

The part of quantum computing that deserves an infrastructure firm's attention this year has
nothing to do with speed. It is what quantum computing already means for the encrypted project
data a firm is holding right now.

More on the research, the real world pilots already running, and what I am watching next, in
this week's issue.

## LinkedIn Post B, two days later

Breaking the encryption protecting a firm's project data only takes a quantum computer that is
good at factoring large numbers, a narrower skill than engineering simulation, and that bar
keeps getting lower.

In 2019 breaking a standard 2048 bit RSA key was estimated to need about 20 million qubits. A
paper published in May 2025 cut that estimate to under a million, a twentyfold drop in six
years.

Google has since set 2029 as its own internal deadline for protecting its systems, explicitly
because adversaries can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once a capable machine
exists. That risk starts the moment a capable machine exists, long before quantum computers
become useful for structural analysis or materials design.

Most AEC firms are not defense contractors or banks, and no regulator is forcing a migration
date on them yet. That is a reasonable reason not to panic. Waiting until a client or an
insurer asks the question first is a different decision entirely.

This week's issue has the full research, including where quantum computing is already showing
real, if narrow, results in structural optimization and materials science.
