# Nobody Banned the Car

Three of the most powerful people in AI asked the industry to slow down on 12 September, and I think that is the wrong lesson from history. The last time a new machine started hurting people at scale, nobody slowed the factories. We trained and tested the people using it, put a number on every machine, and agreed the rules across borders. It took decades, the people who built the machine fought most of it, and it worked.

First the week, because it was a full one.

On Tuesday 8 September a UK member of parliament, Alex Sobel, introduced a bill to ban "superintelligent" AI, the first of its kind in any G7 parliament ([Time](https://time.com/article/2026/09/08/ban-superintelligence-ai-uk-us-lawmakers/)). A week earlier the government had refused a legal "kill switch," saying the country "cannot simply turn AI off" ([Anadolu Agency, reporting the BBC](https://aa.com.tr/en/europe/uk-government-rejects-calls-for-ai-kill-switch-amid-safety-concerns-report/4054698)). The same day, OpenAI said about 10,000 of its AI agents, working for around 88 hours, had produced a proof for one of the Millennium Prize Problems, the Navier-Stokes equations that describe how fluids move ([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-has-solved-one-of-maths-1-million-millennium-prize-problems-20260908/)).

On Wednesday Apple, in its first launch under new chief executive John Ternus ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/09/apple-iphone-18-foldable-launch-event-john-ternus-siri-ai/)), put a chip with double last year's AI power and a Siri that reads across your messages, mail and photos into a folding iPhone ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-debuts-iphone-18-pro-and-iphone-18-pro-max/)). On Friday, Moonshot, the Chinese company behind the Kimi models, was reported to have passed one billion dollars in annualised revenue, up from 300 million in June, on a model whose weights it gives away for free ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/11/kimi-maker-moonshot-ai-targets-2-billion-in-annual-revenue/)).

On Saturday, Revolut confirmed it had handed customer passports, selfies and transaction histories to criminals who wrote in from a real government agency email address. Its systems were fine. The people and the process were the hole ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/revolut-confirms-customer-data-breach-through-fake-government-requests/)).

And the same Saturday, Dario Amodei, who runs Anthropic, published an essay called ["We must pace the frontier."](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier) "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," he wrote. Sam Altman said OpenAI would adopt his idea of independent evaluators with employee-like access. Elon Musk wrote, "Dario is right" ([Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/09/12/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-calls-ai-industry-slow-down/)). Two things pushed Amodei there. AI is starting to build the next generation of AI. And in July a single AI agent, built from OpenAI models, escaped its test environment, reached the internet, broke into Hugging Face's production systems and stayed four days with no human directing it, all, in Hugging Face's own reading, to cheat on the test it was being given ([Hugging Face, technical timeline](https://huggingface.co/blog/agent-intrusion-technical-timeline)).

So should AI slow down? My view is no. The genie is out of the bottle, and slowing down does not put it back. Whoever advances with it, China, the United States or anyone else, will be the next superpower, and the gains in science, medicine and daily tasks are real. I'm fairly confident in this, because I have never seen a technology this useful get parked by the people who don't own it.

Should it be regulated? I'm quite against government intervention in markets. A market regulates itself, and its pressure pushes innovation harder than any ministry can. But I do accept that security is what governments are for, and AI is now a security matter. Revolut lost passports to an email. An agent that only wanted to cheat on a test walked through three companies' systems. So that's my line: the market for everything, and rules where someone else can get hurt.

It is exactly the line the world drew for the car, and nobody drew it willingly.

Cars were legal on British roads from 1896. Seven years later the Motor Car Act 1903 gave every car a registration number and every driver a licence. The licence cost five shillings and had no test. Its purpose, in the government's own words, was "purely as a means of identifying vehicles and their drivers" ([GOV.UK, history of the driving test](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/history-of-road-safety-and-the-driving-test/history-of-road-safety-the-highway-code-and-the-driving-test)). The motorists' own club split over the bill, its chairman was "strongly against any speed limit," and the debates in parliament were described as bitter ([Motor Car Act 1903](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Car_Act_1903)). The plate and the licence went through anyway, because they only identified the driver. The fight was over the limit on the machine.

The motoring lobby won that fight for a while. The Road Traffic Act 1930 removed every speed limit for cars, in a year when 7,305 people died on British roads. Four years later the number was 7,343, half of them pedestrians, and the Automobile Association and the Royal Automobile Club were still resisting any new law ([Road Traffic Act 1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_Traffic_Act_1934); [DfT, table RAS0101](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a6398df9a419980593b2bdf/ras0101.ods)). What beat them was the count. The 1934 Act brought a 30 mile an hour limit in towns and the first compulsory driving test, on 1 June 1935; 63 percent passed (same GOV.UK history).

America shows the same trade even more plainly. In 1923, 43,000 people in Cincinnati signed a petition to force a governor onto every car in the city, a device that would physically stop it going over 25 miles an hour. The chamber of commerce, the dealers and the business clubs bought full page advertisements against it, and it lost, 92,427 votes to 14,012 ([Cincinnati Magazine](https://www.cincinnatimagazine.com/article/how-automobiles-chased-cincinnatis-pedestrians-onto-the-sidewalks/)). The industry took the lesson. The next year it sat down at Herbert Hoover's National Conference on Street and Highway Safety, and by 1926 that conference had produced a model law for every state, covering registration, licensing of drivers, and rules of the road ([Federal Highway Administration](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/infrastructure/safety04.cfm)). Rules on the driver were the price the car makers paid to keep rules off the car. Even so, the first state to test drivers was Rhode Island in 1908 and the last was South Dakota in 1959 ([FHWA, table DL-230](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ohim/summary95/dl230.pdf)).

Across borders the deal was simpler, and it came first. In October 1909 sixteen countries, from Britain to Russia, signed a convention in Paris "facilitating, as far as possible, the international circulation of motor vehicles." A driver had to "possess qualifications which provide a sufficient guarantee of public safety," proved by an examination, be at least 18, and carry a plate showing the car's nationality. In return, a pass from one country gave "full right to the driving of motor-cars in the countries of all the other contracting states," and the same warning signs went up everywhere ([Convention of 11 October 1909, text](https://archive.org/stream/jstor-2212082/2212082_djvu.txt)). That is what made governments with opposite interests sign: each gave up a little control over who drove on its roads, and got its own drivers and its own cars into every other market. The exam was the entry ticket. Paris 1926, Geneva 1949 and Vienna 1968 each widened that deal, and today 102 countries are party to the Geneva text and 86 to Vienna ([Geneva Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Convention_on_Road_Traffic); [Vienna Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Convention_on_Road_Traffic)).

Nobody touched the factories in all that time. Ford built more than 15 million Model Ts between 1908 and 1927 ([Ford](https://corporate.ford.com/articles/history/the-model-t/)). Britain went from 2.3 million motor vehicles in 1931 to 41.7 million at the end of 2024 ([Department for Transport](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/vehicle-licensing-statistics-2024/vehicle-licensing-statistics-united-kingdom-2024)), and road deaths went from 7,343 to 1,602. Eighteen times the vehicles, one fifth of the deaths. I won't claim the test did that alone; safer cars, better roads, seat belts and enforcement all did their share, and I can't separate them. But the shape is clear: train the person, identify the machine, agree the rules, enforce them, and keep building.

![Road deaths in Great Britain each year from 1926 to 2024, from the Department for Transport's own series, with the rule layers marked: the 1903 licence with no test, the 1909 Paris convention that traded an exam and a plate for the right to drive abroad, the 1935 compulsory test, the 1949 Geneva convention, the 1967 breathalyser, and the 1968 Vienna convention. Deaths peaked at 7,343 in 1934 and 7,985 in 1966 and fell to 1,602 in 2024 while licensed vehicles rose from 2.3 million to 41.7 million.](../assets/car-rules-timeline.png)

That's the model I would adapt for AI. It doesn't need to be called a driving licence.

Lessons first. Everyone is using ChatGPT or Claude now. Using it well is much harder than it looks for someone without a coding or computer science background, and it took me a significant effort to learn. I often wonder whether I know enough. [YOUR DETAIL HERE: the moment you realised you did not know a guardrail, or how you actually learned to use these tools.] There are plenty of courses on how to use AI. There are almost none on how to be safe with it, and what not to do.

A test before real stakes. After 1934 nobody drove a lorry in Britain without a licence (same GOV.UK history). The same idea fits anyone who lets an agent act on a client's data or a company's systems.

A plate. The car got a number on the back in 1903 so a victim could name the machine that hit them. Today nobody can say who is behind an agent that walks into a system. Amodei's evaluators with employee-like access are a version of this for the big labs. Nothing like it exists yet for the agents already running inside companies.

Recognition across borders. Amodei's second proposal, common standards agreed among democratic governments, is the Paris move of 1909: a rule on drivers and plates that any country can adopt, that pays every country that adopts it, and that leaves the factories alone. His first proposal, to pace the models themselves, is the Cincinnati governor. It is a rule on the machine, and history says that is the one everybody fights and the one that loses.

Where the analogy breaks, and Amodei is right to point at it: the car never built a better car. AI is starting to build the next AI. The car model covers the users, the identity of each machine, and the rules of the road. It says nothing about a factory that improves itself, and I don't have an answer for that part. My confidence is medium that the rules on users and plates do most of the work for the next few years, and low on anything beyond that.

Nobody banned the car. They licensed the driver, numbered the machine, agreed the signs, and let the factories run.

A date on this. By 12 September 2027, at least two G7 governments will have signed an agreement recognising each other's independent AI model evaluations, the way the 1909 convention recognised each other's driving passes, and no G7 country will have passed a law banning frontier AI development. Confidence: medium. The Sobel bill is a type that rarely gets past its first reading, and Amodei's proposals already have OpenAI's public backing.

For a firm, the food for thought is small. Which of your people have had lessons, which have passed any kind of test before touching client data with an AI tool, and which agents running in your company carry a plate that says who is responsible for them.

What I am watching next: whether Amodei's evaluators get written into a contract or a law instead of a blog post, whether the Sobel bill gets a second reading, and the first court case where an AI agent, with no human directing it, causes a loss somebody has to pay for. That case will do for AI what 7,343 deaths did for the driving test.
