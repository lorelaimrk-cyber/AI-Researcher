# The New Reserve Currency Is Compute

I have been working through Ray Dalio's *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* this month. [YOUR DETAIL HERE: say when you picked it up and what prompted it, so the opening is exact]

The book studies the rise and fall of every major world power over the past five hundred years, the Dutch empire, the British one, the American one we are living inside now, and reduces that history to eight measures that rise and fall together in a repeating cycle: education, competitiveness, innovation and technology, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, financial center strength, and reserve currency status. The useful part, for anyone trying to read the present instead of just the past, is which of those eight moves first. Innovation and technology leadership tends to show up early, often decades before a nation's currency becomes the one the rest of the world settles its trade in. Reserve currency status is closer to the last thing to arrive, and by the time it does, the empire holding it has usually already peaked, since the confidence a reserve currency brings tends to produce the debt and complacency that start the decline.

That ordering is worth sitting with, because it points at where to look for the next shift before it shows up in GDP tables or currency markets: at who is building the infrastructure the era's defining technology needs, and who is willing to pay for it before the payoff is proven. Britain built canals and then railways before it built an empire. The United States built the interstate highway system, partly as a Cold War project, decades before its currency became the one everyone else held in reserve. The infrastructure comes first. The technology it enables comes second. The economic and political power that technology confers comes last, and slowest.

Here is the throughline for this issue. AI is the technology, and the infrastructure being built for it right now, data centers, power generation, and the grid capacity to connect them, is this cycle's canal and interstate. The firms doing the actual engineering and construction of that infrastructure are, project by project, deciding which regions and which companies get to compete in the economy AI is building.

The scale is no longer a forecast. The four largest hyperscalers, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta, are on track to spend a combined 660 to 690 billion dollars on capital expenditure in 2026, up from roughly 380 billion in 2025 ([Futurum Group, AI Capex 2026](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/ai-capex-2026-the-690b-infrastructure-sprint/)). The constraint has moved from money to the grid. PJM, the largest US grid operator, reports that projects reaching commercial operation in 2025 spent more than three years waiting on an interconnection service agreement and another four years after approval before they could connect, a combined average above seven years ([Data Center Knowledge, on PJM's interconnection data](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/energy-power-supply/why-ai-data-center-projects-face-years-of-delays-after-approval)). Projects that clear that queue then wait on hardware. Large power transformers now carry lead times averaging around 128 weeks, and the highest capacity units run to four years, per Wood Mackenzie's tracking (same source). Bloomberg, citing the research firm Sightline Climate, estimates that 30 to 50 percent of the data center capacity US operators planned for 2026 will slip or be cancelled for lack of the transformers and switchgear that actually energize the building, even with the money, land, and chips already in place ([Omni Logistics, citing Bloomberg and Sightline Climate](https://omnilogistics.com/data-center-logistics-2026/)).

This is where the question of who decides stops being a metaphor and becomes an actual regulatory docket. On December 18, 2025, FERC ruled that PJM's tariff had no clear answer for what happens when a data center sits directly behind a power plant's meter to skip the public grid, and ordered PJM, within 60 days, to define three new categories of transmission service spelling out exactly how much of the shared grid a co located load may draw and under what conditions ([Utility Dive, on FERC's PJM co location order](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ferc-pjm-colocation-data-center/808368/)). Amazon had already been through a version of that fight. FERC rejected an earlier version of its arrangement to draw power from Talen Energy's Susquehanna nuclear plant, and the two companies restructured the deal, a 1,920 megawatt agreement phasing in through 2032, as a front of the meter arrangement that does not need FERC's approval at all ([Utility Dive, on the Talen and Amazon Susquehanna agreement](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/talen-amazon-aws-susquehanna-nuclear-data-centert/750440/)). Microsoft took a more direct route still, a 20 year agreement with Constellation Energy to bring Three Mile Island's undamaged reactor back online as the Crane Clean Energy Center, aiming for 2028, pending federal safety review and state permits ([World Nuclear News, on the Crane Clean Energy Center restart](https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/articles/constellation-to-restart-three-mile-island-unit-powering-microsoft)). Contracting for your own power plant is what a company does once it stops trusting the shared grid to answer fast enough.

Even a fully permitted, fully powered project still has to get built, and the physical build is its own bottleneck now. Large transformers and switchgear are oversized freight that cannot move on a standard rail container, and they are competing for a limited supply of heavy haul trucks, engineered rail flatcars, and port capacity built for a different economy's freight (Omni Logistics, same source). The queue, the hardware, and the ports are all places where a decision gets made about whose project moves first.

For a firm that designs, permits, or builds any of this, data centers are already one of the few construction markets still accelerating while the rest of the industry cools. The less obvious part is that the differentiator inside that market has shifted away from design and software capability toward the unglamorous mechanics of grid interconnection, power generation siting, and the logistics of moving oversized equipment to remote sites. That is a different skill set than most engineering firms built their reputations on, and it carries its own exposure: a client base concentrated in a handful of hyperscalers, and growing public friction as local ratepayers watch their own bills move to fund grid upgrades built for someone else's data center.

A date on this. By the end of 2027, at least one other major US grid operator, most likely ERCOT or MISO, will adopt a co located large load framework close to what FERC just ordered PJM to build, because the underlying tension, a deep pocketed customer wanting guaranteed capacity on a grid that ordinary ratepayers are also standing in line for, shows up in every region with a fast growing large load queue. Confidence: medium high. ERCOT's own large load interconnection queue has already grown past 410 gigawatts, mostly data centers and crypto miners, and Texas is under comparable pressure to answer the same question PJM was just forced to answer ([RTO Insider, on ERCOT's large load queue](https://www.rtoinsider.com/129421-ercot-large-load-requests-soar-again/)).

What I am watching next is whether any engineering or infrastructure consultancy starts reporting a distinct power and grid interconnection backlog in its own results, the way hyperscalers now report a cloud backlog. That would be the sign that this work has become a named practice line in its own right, separate from general infrastructure revenue, and that the firms doing it understand what they are actually holding.
