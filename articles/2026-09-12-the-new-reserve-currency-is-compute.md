# The New Reserve Currency Is Compute

I have been working through Ray Dalio's *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* this month. [YOUR DETAIL HERE: say when you picked it up and what prompted it, so the opening is exact]

The book studies the rise and fall of every major world power over the past five hundred years, the Dutch empire, the British one, the American one we are living inside now, and reduces that history to eight measures that rise and fall together in a repeating cycle: education, competitiveness, innovation and technology, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, financial center strength, and reserve currency status. The useful part, for anyone trying to read the present instead of just the past, is which of those eight moves first. Innovation and technology leadership tends to show up early, often decades before a nation's currency becomes the one the rest of the world settles its trade in. Reserve currency status is closer to the last thing to arrive, and by the time it does, the empire holding it has usually already peaked, since the confidence a reserve currency brings tends to produce the debt and complacency that start the decline.

That ordering is worth sitting with, because it points at where to look for the next shift before it shows up in GDP tables or currency markets: at who is building the infrastructure the era's defining technology needs, and who is willing to pay for it before the payoff is proven. Britain built canals and then railways before it built an empire. The United States built the interstate highway system, partly as a Cold War project, decades before its currency became the one everyone else held in reserve. The infrastructure comes first. The technology it enables comes second. The economic and political power that technology confers comes last, and slowest.

Here is the throughline for this issue. AI is the technology, and the infrastructure being built for it right now, data centers, power plants, and the grid capacity to connect them, is this cycle's canal and interstate. The firms doing the actual engineering and construction of that infrastructure are, project by project, deciding which regions and which companies get to compete in the economy AI is building.

The scale of the spend is no longer a forecast. The four biggest technology companies building AI infrastructure, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta, are on track to spend a combined 660 to 690 billion dollars on it in 2026, up from roughly 380 billion last year ([Futurum Group, AI Capex 2026](https://futurumgroup.com/insights/ai-capex-2026-the-690b-infrastructure-sprint/)). The constraint has moved from money to the grid. The operator of the largest power grid in the country, covering most of the mid Atlantic states, reports that projects finishing construction in 2025 waited more than seven years on average just to get approved for and connected to the grid ([Data Center Knowledge, on grid interconnection delays](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/energy-power-supply/why-ai-data-center-projects-face-years-of-delays-after-approval)). Even approved projects then wait on equipment. The large transformers that step grid power down to something a building can use now take more than two years to arrive on average, and the biggest ones take up to four (same source). Put together, one estimate puts 30 to 50 percent of the data center capacity planned for 2026 in the US at risk of slipping or being cancelled simply because that equipment has not arrived, even where the money, land, and permits are already in place ([Omni Logistics, citing Bloomberg and Sightline Climate](https://omnilogistics.com/data-center-logistics-2026/)).

That wait is why some of the largest technology companies are choosing to skip the shared grid altogether. Microsoft has agreed to bring the Three Mile Island nuclear plant back online specifically to power its own data centers rather than wait years for a grid connection ([World Nuclear News, on the Crane Clean Energy Center restart](https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/articles/constellation-to-restart-three-mile-island-unit-powering-microsoft)). It is, in effect, buying its own power plant because it no longer trusts the shared grid to answer fast enough, and it will not be the last company to make that choice.

For an engineering or consulting firm, this changes what actually wins the work. A few years ago, design quality was the differentiator. Now it is speed, specifically how fast a firm can get a project through permitting, secure its power, and connect to the grid, since that timeline decides whether a client's data center opens on schedule. The firms already picking up outsized work in this space share real, hands on expertise in grid interconnection and energy permitting, the kind that lets them tell a client honestly, before ground is broken, how many years a project will actually take. That is a genuinely different skill to build than most engineering firms have historically sold. The real question for a firm reading this is whether its own energy and permitting practice could compete on that basis today, or whether it is still selling almost entirely on design.

It also comes with real exposure. A handful of technology companies now account for an outsized share of this work, and the public mood is starting to turn as ordinary electricity customers watch their own bills rise to help fund grid upgrades built mainly for someone else's data center.

A date on this. By the end of 2027, at least one other major US grid region, most likely Texas, will be forced to write new rules for how these private power deals get handled, following the same path federal energy regulators already forced onto the mid Atlantic grid in December 2025 ([Utility Dive, on the new federal rules for the mid Atlantic grid](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ferc-pjm-colocation-data-center/808368/)), because the same pressure, more demand for guaranteed power than the shared grid can supply, is showing up wherever large amounts of data centers are being built. Confidence: medium high. Texas's own queue of large power users waiting to connect has already passed 410 gigawatts, most of it data centers, and is under the same strain ([RTO Insider, on Texas's grid queue](https://www.rtoinsider.com/129421-ercot-large-load-requests-soar-again/)).

What I am watching next is whether any engineering or infrastructure firm starts explicitly selling grid interconnection speed as its own service, the way hyperscalers now report a cloud backlog as its own number. That would be the sign that this has become a real, named differentiator, not just something folded into general infrastructure work.
