## LinkedIn Post A, publish day

Nobody banned the car. They licensed the driver, numbered the machine, agreed the signs, and let the factories run.

On 12 September Dario Amodei asked the AI industry to slow down. Sam Altman and Elon Musk agreed with him. I think that is the wrong lesson from history.

Britain let cars on the road in 1896. In 1903 it gave every car a number and every driver a licence with no test. The test came in 1935, after 7,343 deaths in one year. Sixteen countries had already agreed in 1909 that an exam and a plate bought the right to drive on each other's roads.

Over that whole period nobody slowed the car makers. Ford built 15 million Model Ts. Britain went from 2.3 million vehicles to 41.7 million, and road deaths fell to 1,602.

The rules went on the person, the plate and the border. That is the model I would adapt for AI, and it does not need to be called a licence. This week's issue lays out the dates and the numbers.

## LinkedIn Post B, two days later

In 1923, 43,000 people in Cincinnati signed a petition to put a governor on every car, a device that would physically stop it going over 25 miles an hour. The dealers and the chamber of commerce bought full page advertisements against it. It lost, 92,427 votes to 14,012.

The next year the car industry sat down at Herbert Hoover's road safety conference and helped write a model law for every state: registration, driver licensing, rules of the road.

Rules on the driver were the price the industry paid to keep rules off the car.

That is the split in this week's AI debate. Amodei's proposal for common standards among democracies is a rule on drivers and plates. His proposal to pace the models themselves is the Cincinnati governor, and history says that is the one everybody fights.

The one place the car story cannot help is the part he is most worried about: the car never built a better car. I say so in the piece.
