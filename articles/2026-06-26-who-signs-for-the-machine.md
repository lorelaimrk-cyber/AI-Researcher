# Who signs for the machine

Insurers have started pricing AI work on the one human who can vouch for it. That human is the senior the training pyramid has quietly stopped making.

## The claim

On January 1, 2026, Verisk's ISO released two endorsements, CG 40 47 and CG 40 48, that let general liability policies exclude losses "arising out of" generative AI, one of the broadest causal phrases in policy drafting ([Independent Agent](https://www.independentagent.com/vu_resource/verisk-to-roll-out-new-general-liability-exclusions-for-generative-ai-exposures/); [actuary.info](https://actuary.info/insights/iso-cg-40-47-genai-gl-exclusion-premium-adequacy-2026)). The work that stays insured is the work a licensed professional reviews, revises, and seals. So the value of an engineering firm is concentrating into the person who can stand behind what a model produced. That person is made by years of doing the junior work the model now does. The same firms that are thinning the junior ranks to capture AI's cost savings are unmaking the reviewer their insurance now requires. The safeguard and the pipeline are running in opposite directions, and most firms have not noticed they own both clocks.

## What happened

Two stories landed in the same window and almost no one read them together.

The first is an insurance story. Verisk's January endorsements gave carriers a clean way to carve generative AI out of coverage, and the carriers moved fast. By April 2026, W.R. Berkley, Chubb, Travelers, Berkshire Hathaway, and Cincinnati Financial had filed to adopt the new exclusions or their own AI language, and state regulators had approved more than 80% of the filings ([actuary.info](https://actuary.info/insights/iso-cg-40-47-genai-gl-exclusion-premium-adequacy-2026)). The same shift reached design professional errors and omissions policies, the cover an engineering firm cannot work without ([Financial Content](https://markets.financialcontent.com/wedbush/article/marketersmedia-2026-1-16-insurance-carriers-add-ai-exclusions-to-design-professional-e-and-o-policies)). The line carriers are drawing is about review. A specification a model drafted that a licensed engineer reads, corrects, and seals sits where professionally reviewed work has always sat. A deliverable a model produced that passed through with a light human touch is the one that triggers a coverage fight. One insurer already saw the failure mode in practice: a consulting firm shipped a report built on Azure OpenAI that carried non-existent references and fabricated court quotes, and it ended in corrections and a partial refund ([Risk & Insurance](https://riskandinsurance.com/ai-governance-failures-expose-organizations-to-professional-liability-risks/)).

The second is a talent story. Mark Russinovich, Microsoft's Azure CTO, and Scott Hanselman, a Microsoft vice president, published a paper in Communications of the ACM in April 2026 arguing that AI puts a drag on early-career engineers while it lifts senior productivity, and that the combination is collapsing the pipeline that turns juniors into seniors ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/04/junior-developer-pipeline-crisis/)). They cite a Harvard finding that employment of 22 to 25 year-olds in AI-exposed jobs fell by roughly 13% after GPT-4, and report junior hiring down 67% since 2022. Their fix borrows from medicine: a preceptor model, where a junior learns beside a senior on real work. Hanselman put the bar plainly. "Just as a nurse needs to prove clinical readiness, engineers need to do the same to earn the title."

## Why it matters to a firm like yours

A consultancy is a machine that converts senior judgment into sealed deliverables. For a century the seal was cheap to supply because the firm produced its own seniors as a byproduct of doing the work. Juniors did the takeoffs, the calc checks, the first drafts, and the document review, and a decade of that made the judgment that later signed the drawing. The work was the school.

AI now does the schoolwork. The takeoff, the first draft, the document review, the boilerplate calculation, these are the tasks a model does in seconds, and they are exactly the reps that used to build a reviewer. Here is the part the insurance change makes sharp. The model lowered the cost of producing the deliverable and, in the same motion, raised the value of the person who can vouch for it, because vouching is now what keeps the work insured. The firm faces two prices moving apart. The cost of a draft falls toward zero. The scarcity of a credible signature climbs. A firm that reads only the first number, and trims its juniors to bank the saving, is selling the second number short without seeing the trade.

I am fairly confident the direction is right, and I want to be honest about where it is thin. The hard numbers, the 13% and the 67%, come from software ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/04/junior-developer-pipeline-crisis/)). The work I write about is stamped engineering, which differs in ways that matter. AEC carries licensure, site time, and physical liability that already slow how fast a junior can be cut, so the effect should arrive later and softer in a structural engineering practice than in a software team. The mechanism transfers even where the magnitude does not. When the first rungs of the ladder are the rungs a model now climbs for free, the people at the top get older and no one new arrives behind them.

## The benefit case

The constraint is also the opening. A firm that treats the senior review as the product, and says so to clients and to its insurer, turns a coverage problem into a reason to be hired. Insurable AI work is a real differentiator in a market where every competitor has the same models and not every competitor can prove a qualified human stood behind the output. The firm that rebuilds an apprenticeship around that proof, putting juniors to work as critics of model output rather than producers of first drafts, trains the exact skill the new regime pays for, which is the judgment to catch what the model got wrong. Russinovich and Hanselman's preceptor model is a workable shape for it, and a midsize firm can run it on one team before the market forces the question.

## The risk case

The honest counter is that this fear is fashionable and may be oversold. If the models keep improving, the named reviewer becomes a light touch on reliable output, the scarcity never bites, and a firm that hoarded expensive juniors on a pipeline theory will have carried dead weight while leaner rivals booked the margin. The insurance language cuts in more than one direction too. Carriers wrote the exclusions to limit their own exposure, and a firm cannot assume that a human seal restores full cover, because "arising out of" is broad enough to reach work a person reviewed ([actuary.info](https://actuary.info/insights/iso-cg-40-47-genai-gl-exclusion-premium-adequacy-2026)). I hold the timing loosely. The structural pull is real. The quarter it shows up in a given firm's hiring plan is not something the current evidence can date.

## The throughline

> The model made the junior's work cheap and the senior's signature scarce. A firm that keeps trimming the juniors is selling the signatures it will need in ten years to fund the margin it wants this quarter.

## A prediction

> Prediction (2026-06-26): By the end of 2027, at least one of the large global engineering consultancies (Arup, AECOM, Jacobs, WSP, Stantec) will publicly name a formal program to credential or apprentice the people who review and seal AI-assisted delivery, framed around who is qualified to check model output. Confidence: medium.

## What I am watching next

The signal that matters most is whether insurers move from a vague human in the loop to a named, credentialed reviewer with a defined scope, because that is the moment the scarcity gets priced directly. I am also watching for the first AEC firm to rebuild its junior training around reviewing model output rather than producing first drafts, and for hiring data on the entry cohort drawn from stamped engineering rather than software. The finding that would change my mind is steady junior hiring across AEC through 2026, which would tell me licensure and site work are protecting the pipeline that software could not protect.
