## LinkedIn Post A, publish day

Two years ago, 32 people at a law firm sent a few hundred AI prompts a month. Today, nearly
2,200 people at that same firm send more than 282,000 a month.

That is not a story about people finally waking up. It took two years and a network of
internal champions convincing colleagues one conversation at a time.

Which raises the real question for any firm that has bought AI seats: does a rising login or
token count mean the work got better, or just that more people are busy with the tool? Those
are two different facts, and most license dashboards only show one of them.

Microsoft's own 2026 research splits AI users into three groups, and about half are still
described as finding their footing two years in. That is not a failure rate. That is what a
real adoption curve looks like.

The firms that separate from the pack will not be the ones with the highest login numbers.
They will be the ones that also check whether the work got better. More on what that
measurement could look like in this week's issue.

## LinkedIn Post B, two days later

Once a firm's utilization already covers its cost, more hours logged stop making it more
profitable on their own. David Maister said that about billable hours thirty years ago.

Firms buying AI seats are now facing the identical math, mostly without realizing it.

Economist Charles Goodhart made a related point in 1975, writing about monetary policy: once a
number becomes the target, it tends to stop measuring what it originally measured.

Track logins or token counts as the success metric, and a firm will get more logins and token
counts. That much is close to guaranteed. Whether it also gets better work is a separate
question, and most firms do not yet have a clean way to answer it.

The interesting firms right now are not the ones with the busiest AI seats. They are the rare
few starting to check whether busy seats produced anything worth measuring.
