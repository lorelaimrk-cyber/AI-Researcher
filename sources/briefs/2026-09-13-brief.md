## The car: why the rules came, how long, what they prevented, how they went global

**1896 to 1903, Britain.** The Locomotives on Highways Act 1896 let motor cars on the road. The
Motor Car Act 1903 (Royal Assent 14 Aug 1903, in force 1 Jan 1904) introduced compulsory
registration numbers and a driving licence costing five shillings, minimum age 17, no test.
Government's own history: licences were introduced "purely as a means of identifying vehicles and
their drivers."
Sources: [GOV.UK, history of road safety, the Highway Code and the driving test](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/history-of-road-safety-and-the-driving-test/history-of-road-safety-the-highway-code-and-the-driving-test);
[Motor Car Act 1903 text](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Edw7/3/36/contents/enacted);
[Wikipedia, Motor Car Act 1903](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Car_Act_1903).

**1903 to 1959, United States.** FHWA table DL-230: first state licence laws Massachusetts and
Missouri 1903; first examination Rhode Island 1908; last state licence law South Dakota 1954; last
examination South Dakota 1959. By 1930 only 24 states required a licence and 15 an exam.
Sources: [FHWA, table DL-230](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ohim/summary95/dl230.pdf);
[Jalopnik on the 50 year rollout](https://www.jalopnik.com/2238062/50-years-for-drivers-licenses-to-become-mandatory-in-every-state/).

**The factories never slowed.** Ford built more than 15 million Model Ts between 1908 and May 1927.
UK: 2.3 million motor vehicles in 1931 (GOV.UK history); 41.7 million licensed vehicles at end
2024 (DfT).
Sources: [Ford, the Model T](https://corporate.ford.com/articles/history/the-model-t/);
[DfT, vehicle licensing statistics 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/vehicle-licensing-statistics-2024/vehicle-licensing-statistics-united-kingdom-2024).

**Deaths, Great Britain, DfT table RAS0101 (killed / all casualties).** 1926: 4,886 / 139,000.
1930: 7,305 / 185,000. 1934: 7,343 / 239,000. 1935: 6,502 / 228,000. 1941: 9,169 (wartime).
1966: 7,985 / 392,000. 1967: 7,319. 2004: 3,221. 2023: 1,624. 2024: 1,602 / 128,272.
Source: [DfT, RAS0101](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a6398df9a419980593b2bdf/ras0101.ods),
from [Road safety statistics data tables](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistical-data-sets/reported-road-accidents-vehicles-and-casualties-tables-for-great-britain).

**The layers.** 1930 Road Traffic Act (licensing of public service vehicles). 1931 first Highway
Code. 1934 Road Traffic Act (lorry driver licences; the driving test). Compulsory test from 1 June
1935, cost 7s 6d, pass rate 63 percent, first passer Mr R Beere. Drink drive law and breathalyser
8 October 1967. Source: GOV.UK history page above.
Seat belts 1983: not confirmed on a primary page this session; left out of the article.

**Across borders.** 1926 Paris International Convention relative to Motor Traffic: first
international driving document. 1949 Geneva Convention on Road Traffic (signed 19 Sept 1949, in
force 26 March 1952, 102 parties as of March 2025): mutual recognition of driving permits, plates in
Latin letters, country sign. 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic (signed 8 Nov 1968, in force
21 May 1977, 86 parties as of Oct 2022): permit only after theory and practical exams; parties must
recognise each other's licences; sister Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals the same year.
Sources: [Wikipedia, Geneva Convention on Road Traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Convention_on_Road_Traffic);
[Wikipedia, Vienna Convention on Road Traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Convention_on_Road_Traffic);
[Wikipedia, International Driving Permit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Driving_Permit).
Uncertain: UNECE's own page returned 403; the Wikipedia pages cite the UN treaty collection.

## Who fought the rules, and what made them accept (added after Lorela's mid-session question)

**Britain, 1903.** The Automobile Club split over the Motor Car Bill; its chairman Roger Wallace was
"strongly against any speed limit" and called the moderate Montagu a "traitor"; debates were
"bitter." Registration and the licence passed; the fight was over the speed limit on the machine.
Source: [Wikipedia, Motor Car Act 1903](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Car_Act_1903) (cites Hansard).

**Britain, 1930 to 1934.** The Road Traffic Act 1930 "controversially removed all speed limits for
motorcars" in a year with a record 7,305 deaths. In 1934: 7,343 deaths, 231,603 injuries, half the
deaths pedestrians. The AA and RAC "resisted further legislation." The 1934 Act reintroduced 30 mph
in built-up areas and made the test compulsory from 1 June 1935. Pedestrians' Association founded
August 1929 on the other side.
Source: [Wikipedia, Road Traffic Act 1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_Traffic_Act_1934);
deaths from DfT RAS0101 (7,305 in 1930, 7,343 in 1934).

**United States, Cincinnati 1923.** From June 1922 an Initiative Committee gathered signatures
(over 43,000, more than 10 percent of the city) for an ordinance requiring a "mechanical speed
regulating device" holding every car under 25 mph. The Chamber of Commerce, Academy of Medicine,
Businessmen's Club and Central Labor Council spent 7,365.50 dollars on full page advertisements;
Congressman Alfred G. Allen led the opposition. Result: 14,012 for, 92,427 against. Cincinnati
deaths kept rising: 103 that year, 157 by 1929, 201 by 1934.
Source: [Cincinnati Magazine](https://www.cincinnatimagazine.com/article/how-automobiles-chased-cincinnatis-pedestrians-onto-the-sidewalks/).
Secondary: [Streets Are For Everyone](https://www.streetsareforeveryone.org/blog/why-does-the-auto-industry-oppose-safety-improvements-pt2)
(dealers sent letters to car owners; by the 1924 Hoover conference the industry's line was human
error, not vehicle design). Peter Norton, Fighting Traffic, is the academic source; not fetched.

**United States, Hoover conferences 1924 and 1926.** Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover chaired
the National Conference on Street and Highway Safety in 1924; the 1926 conference produced the
Uniform Vehicle Code, "three separate acts dealing with registration and certificate of title, with
licensing of operators and chauffeurs, and with rules governing the operation of vehicles on
highways." Modern state motor vehicle departments grew out of it.
Source: [FHWA, The Federal Role in Highway Safety, chapter 4](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/infrastructure/safety04.cfm).
Uncertain: attendee list by sector not confirmed from a primary page; the article says only that the
industry "sat down" at the conference, which the secondary sources support.

**Paris, 11 October 1909.** Convention with respect to the International Circulation of Motor
Vehicles, signed by Great Britain, Germany, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Bulgaria, Spain, France,
Greece, Italy, Monaco, Montenegro, Netherlands, Portugal, Romania, Russia and Serbia, "facilitating,
as far as possible, the international circulation of motor vehicles." Article 2: the driver "must
possess qualifications which provide a sufficient guarantee of public safety," authorised after an
examination, minimum age 18. Article 3: international travelling passes valid one year giving "full
right to the driving of motor-cars in the countries of all the other contracting states." Article 4:
nationality plate. Article 8: standard warning signs.
Source: [Convention text, archive.org (JSTOR scan)](https://archive.org/stream/jstor-2212082/2212082_djvu.txt).

**The pattern.** Rules on the driver and the plate passed; rules on the machine (speed limits,
governors) were fought and mostly lost until the death count forced them. Cross border rules were
accepted because the exam and the plate were the entry ticket to every other country's roads and
market. Amodei's "common standards among democracies" is the 1909 move; "pace the models" is the
Cincinnati governor.

## The pattern (first pass)

Every car rule from 1903 to 1968 was aimed at the driver, the identity of the vehicle, or the road.
None was aimed at how fast engineers improved the car. Rules arrived in layers, each after a harm
you could count, and they only crossed borders once enough countries had drivers who wanted to
drive abroad. Amodei's own three proposals map onto that history almost exactly, except for the one
worry the car never had: the machine improving itself.
