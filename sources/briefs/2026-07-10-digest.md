# Research digest: 2026-07-10

Topic requested: is scaling with AI a slow giveaway of a firm's IP, is sitting out a slower way
to lose, where the middle ground sits, and how consultancies reposition. Pulled by direct web
research (not gather.py) since this is a bespoke thesis, not a feed-ranked story. Every entry
below is a primary or named source, fetched directly.

## Pillar 3: Risk, liability, and governance (primary)

1. **Autodesk's Terms of Use bar customers from training AI on their own output**
   - Source: Autodesk Terms of Use, via AEC Magazine, "Contract killers: how EULAs are
     shifting power from users to shareholders" | 2026
   - Link: https://aecmag.com/business/contract-killers-how-eulas-are-shifting-power-from-users-to-shareholders/
   - Fact: Since May 2018, Autodesk's ToU has prohibited using software "Output" to train "any
     machine learning or artificial intelligence algorithm." The clause is broad enough to
     reach a firm's own design history and IFC exports. Training is permitted only inside
     Autodesk's own cloud (Autodesk Platform Services). Dassault Systèmes separately demands
     10% of turnover plus financial disclosure from third-party developers building on
     Solidworks.
   - Why it matters: A firm does not have to leak data to a chatbot to lose control of its own
     IP. The CAD vendor it already pays can contractually own the right to decide whether the
     firm's own drawings may train the firm's own model. This is pillar 3 and pillar 1 at once:
     it is a rights question and a margin question, because the same data an in-house AI moat
     would be built from is fenced off by the tool used to create it.
   - Uncertain: Autodesk states the intent is to stop reverse-engineering its own models, not to
     block internal use, and enforcement mechanics are not public. Worth flagging as contested
     interpretation, not settled law.

2. **Samsung: the case study that ran the whole arc in three years**
   - Source: Forbes (2023-05-02); CIO Dive; Dark Reading (original leak); reporting on Samsung's
     2026 reversal
   - Links: https://www.forbes.com/sites/siladityaray/2023/05/02/samsung-bans-chatgpt-and-other-chatbots-for-employees-after-sensitive-code-leak/
     ; https://www.darkreading.com/vulnerabilities-threats/samsung-engineers-sensitive-data-chatgpt-warnings-ai-use-workplace
   - Fact: In March 2023, Samsung engineers pasted proprietary semiconductor source code, test
     sequences, and a confidential meeting transcript into ChatGPT within three weeks of an
     internal ban being lifted. Samsung reinstated a full ban within five weeks. A post-incident
     survey found 65% of employees "seriously concerned" about genAI security. In June 2026,
     Samsung signed a new agreement with OpenAI and is rolling out ChatGPT Enterprise and Codex
     to its Device eXperience division, this time under a governance framework with real-time
     data loss prevention and usage monitoring.
   - Why it matters: This is the middle ground made concrete. Samsung did not choose "all in" or
     "all out." It banned, then re-entered three years later with contract terms, monitoring,
     and an enterprise agreement instead of consumer tool access. That sequence, not a single
     decision, is the realistic shape of the answer to the user's question.
   - Uncertain: No public data yet on outcomes of the 2026 governed rollout; too recent to grade.

3. **The scale of the everyday leak, not the headline incident**
   - Source: Cyberhaven Labs research, cited via Cyberhaven's own blog and multiple secondary
     write-ups | data reported through 2024 to 2026
   - Link: https://www.cyberhaven.com/blog/insider-threats-in-the-age-of-ai
   - Fact: Cyberhaven's telemetry across roughly 1.6 million workers found that by March 2024,
     4.7% of employees had pasted company data into AI tools at least once, with 11% of what
     they pasted classified as confidential, and volumes up 485% year over year. A separate,
     more recent Cyberhaven figure states 39.7% of all AI interactions now involve sensitive
     data, though the methodology and date for that specific number were not fully retrievable
     from the source fetched and need to be checked against Cyberhaven's original report before
     it is quoted in the piece.
   - Why it matters: The Samsung story reads as an anomaly. This data says the anomaly is the
     norm at much smaller scale, continuously, inside almost every firm that has not built a
     sanctioned alternative. The risk is not the dramatic leak. It is the accumulation of small
     ones with no sanctioned channel to prevent them.
   - Uncertain: Flagged above. Needs a direct pull of Cyberhaven's original report before using
     the 39.7% figure in print.

4. **January 1, 2026: insurers and contracts start pricing the data question, not just the
   liability one**
   - Source: Verisk (ISO endorsements CG 40 47 / CG 40 48); Dan Cumberland Labs, "AI Clauses in
     Engineering Services Contracts: What Changed January 2026"
   - Link: https://dancumberlandlabs.com/blog/contract-engineering-services-inc/
   - Fact: Alongside the AI liability exclusions covered in the June 26 issue, mid-market AEC
     engineering services agreements are now adding a second, distinct clause family: data
     ownership and AI training restrictions, sitting alongside AI use disclosure, prior written
     consent for new tools, IP allocation for AI-assisted output, and audit rights. 61% of large
     architecture firms (50+ staff) report daily AI use versus 42% of midsize and 27% of small
     firms. AIA B102 and EJCDC E-500, the standard-form contracts the industry actually signs,
     remain silent on AI in their 2026 editions.
   - Why it matters: The contract, not the tool, is where "did we give away our IP" gets decided
     in practice. A firm negotiating a training-restriction clause into its own engagement
     letters is doing the opposite of what it does when it pastes a spec into a consumer chatbot.
     Same technology, opposite IP outcome, and the standard-form contracts everyone still uses
     have not caught up to either.
   - Uncertain: This is one law firm's tracking of mid-market contract language, not a
     industry-wide survey; treat the 8-clause taxonomy as illustrative, not universal practice
     yet.

5. **Zero data retention becomes a sales pitch, not just a technical setting**
   - Source: Multiple vendor and legal-AI sources converging on the same claim, most directly
     articulated at joist.ai and neuraltrust.ai | 2026
   - Link: https://www.joist.ai/post/our-zero-data-retention-zdr-agreements-with-leading-llm-providers
   - Fact: Firms serving regulated or privacy-sensitive clients are increasingly citing a
     published, named zero-data-retention agreement with their underlying model provider
     (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) as a procurement differentiator, stacked with SOC 3 and GDPR
     alignment. The distinction buyers are told to check for: whether the guarantee is enforced
     at the infrastructure level or is only a contract clause.
   - Why it matters: This is the clearest example of the "reposition" half of the question. A
     firm can turn the same underlying risk (data leaving the building) into a stated,
     auditable commitment it sells to the client, rather than a liability it hopes nobody asks
     about.
   - Uncertain: This trend is best documented in legal AI vendor marketing so far; harder
     evidence of AEC or management-consulting firms making the same claim publicly is thinner
     and worth a direct search before the draft ships.

## Pillar 1: Economics of billable expertise

6. **AECOM bought the AI instead of feeding a vendor**
   - Source: AEC Magazine; ENR (Engineering News-Record); Verdantix | deal announced 2025-11-25
   - Links: https://aecmag.com/news/aecom-acquires-ai-start-up-consigli/ ;
     https://www.enr.com/articles/62754-why-aecom-acquired-norwegian-ai-startup-consiglivisionary-deal-or-part-of-the-hype-cycle
   - Fact: AECOM paid $390 million (about 4 billion NOK) for Consigli, a Norwegian startup whose
     "Autonomous Engineer" platform automates MEP load calculations, clash-free BIM, and
     tender-document prep. Consigli's founder, Janne Aas-Jakobsen, becomes AECOM's Head of AI
     Engineering. Commentary from ENR and Verdantix frames this as unusual: normally the AEC
     software vendors buy the AI startups, not their customers.
   - Why it matters: This is the cleanest data point for "the middle ground is not free." AECOM
     did not decide between using a vendor's AI and holding its IP close. It spent $390 million
     to own the model outright rather than feed a third party's, converting an expense line into
     an owned, sellable capability. Most firms in the reader's size range cannot write that
     check, which is exactly why the question of a middle path matters more for them, not less.
   - Uncertain: Too recent to have delivery outcomes. ENR's own coverage is explicitly split on
     whether this is visionary or hype-cycle spending; worth citing that ambivalence rather than
     treating the deal as a proven strategy.

7. **The AI labs are becoming their own customers' competitors, and funding the consultancies at
   the same time**
   - Source: "Your Vendor Is Your Competitor," Aspiring for Intelligence (Substack) | 2026
   - Link: https://aspiringforintelligence.substack.com/p/your-vendor-is-your-competitor
   - Fact: Within weeks of each other in 2026: Anthropic formed a $1.5 billion joint venture
     with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs to embed engineers directly inside
     mid-sized companies. OpenAI raised $4 billion at a $14 billion valuation for a Deployment
     Company and acquired the consulting firm Tomoro, which brought roughly 150
     forward-deployed engineers. Google Cloud committed $750 million to fund AI work by its own
     consulting partners, named as Accenture, Deloitte, and McKinsey. The piece's framing
     stat: for every dollar spent on AI software, six are spent on services, which is the pool
     the labs are now reaching for directly.
   - Why it matters: This complicates the "differentiate" half of the question. The largest
     consultancies are not just competing with AI, some are being directly capitalized by the
     same labs whose tools raise the IP question in the first place. A mid-size firm's
     differentiation strategy has to account for a market where its biggest competitors and its
     AI vendors are increasingly the same balance sheet.
   - Uncertain: This is one analyst's synthesis piece, not a primary filing for each deal. The
     dollar figures should be checked against each company's own announcement before quoting them
     as firm numbers in the draft, though the pattern across three labs in one window is itself
     the story.

8. **McKinsey: the model is table stakes, proprietary data is the only durable moat left**
   - Source: McKinsey QuantumBlack, "From AI table stakes to AI advantage: Building competitive
     moats" | 2026
   - Link: https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/from-ai-table-stakes-to-ai-advantage-building-competitive-moats
   - Fact: McKinsey's framing: deploying frontier models is now necessary to compete but confers
     no lasting edge because it is available to everyone. The durable advantage comes from
     proprietary data sets competitors cannot replicate, workflows nobody else has, and
     institutional knowledge baked into the firm. Companies that "genuinely rewire" around AI
     show EBITDA improvements of 10 to 30%, averaging near 20%, in McKinsey's data.
   - Why it matters: This is the direct answer to "where is the middle ground" from the most
     mainstream possible source. The firm-level choice McKinsey describes is not adopt or
     refuse. It is whether a firm's own project history becomes a closed-loop asset the firm
     owns, or raw material it hands a shared vendor model for free, undifferentiated benefit.
   - Uncertain: McKinsey is not a disinterested party. It sells the rewiring engagements it is
     describing. The EBITDA figure should be attributed explicitly as McKinsey's own claim, not
     presented as independent.

9. **The AEC-specific version of the moat argument: the ontology is the lock-in**
   - Source: AEC Foundry, "AI Can't Read Your Drawings: Inside the Race to Build AEC's Knowledge
     Layer" | 2026
   - Link: https://www.aecfoundry.com/blog/ai-can-t-read-your-drawings---inside-the-race-to-build-aec-s-knowledge-layer
   - Fact: The piece frames a 2026 to 2027 strategic question directly: does the AI layer over
     AEC data get owned by software vendors, or by the engineering and construction firms
     themselves? It cites 41% of the construction workforce reaching retirement age by 2031,
     roughly $177.5 billion a year in labor waste from non-optimal activity, and six contech
     startups raising $126 million in early 2026 aimed specifically at document and drawing
     data, not jobsite hardware. It names the AECOM/Consigli deal as the clearest early signal
     of firms trying to own that layer rather than rent it, and argues that "proprietary
     drawing parsing" is a 12 to 18 month lead at best before open benchmarks close the gap,
     while a firm's own knowledge graph, its project-specific ontology, is the asset that
     survives retirements and does not get commoditized on the same timeline.
   - Why it matters: This gives the newsletter's structural, firm-as-a-machine lens a concrete
     mechanism: the moat is not the model, it is the labeled, structured history of a specific
     firm's own projects, and that is exactly the material an unrestricted AI tool contract can
     quietly extract.
   - Uncertain: This is a single AEC-tech blog's synthesis, well-sourced on the AECOM deal but
     thinner on independent verification of its own retirement and labor-waste figures; worth a
     direct check of the $177.5B and 41% figures against a primary labor-market source before
     they run unattributed.

10. **The adoption gap is real, but "pilot purgatory," not refusal, is where most firms sit**
    - Source: Deltek Clarity A&E Industry Study (via Building Design + Construction); Bluebeam
      2025 AEC AI survey, aggregated in Dan Cumberland Labs, "AI Adoption Curve: Where AEC Firms
      Stand in 2025 to 2026"
    - Link: https://dancumberlandlabs.com/blog/ai-adoption-curve/
    - Fact: 53% of AEC firms use some AI tool, but only 27% use it for automation, problem
      solving, or decision making, and under 10% put tech budget toward AI training. 63% of
      firms are described as stuck in "pilot purgatory," unable to scale a pilot into measured
      results. Firms that got past pilots did see returns: 68% saved at least $50,000 and 46%
      recovered 500 to 1,000 hours, roughly three to six months of team capacity. Only 18% of
      professional services firms track AI ROI at all.
    - Why it matters: This is the strongest evidence against the "all in or all out" framing the
      reader is worried about. The real population is barely at either pole. Most firms are
      already exposed to the leakage risk (they use tools) without having captured the
      productivity case (they have not scaled past pilots), which is close to the worst of both
      worlds rather than a deliberate middle ground.
    - Uncertain: Original survey sample sizes and methodology for Bluebeam and Deltek were not
      independently pulled; both are vendor or vendor-adjacent research, so the specific
      percentages should be attributed by name rather than treated as neutral industry consensus.

11. **The broader enterprise version of the same gap**
    - Source: McKinsey, State of AI reporting, aggregated via multiple 2026 secondary summaries

    - Link: https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/tech-forward/state-of-ai-trust-in-2026-shifting-to-the-agentic-era
    - Fact: 88% of organizations now use AI in at least one business function, but only about 6%

    - Uncertain: This entry is built from secondary summaries of McKinsey's underlying survey; a

## Pillar 5: Clients and the market

12. **Clients have started scoring AI governance in the RFP itself**
    - Source: Dan Cumberland Labs (same piece as item 4, different section)
    - Link: https://dancumberlandlabs.com/blog/contract-engineering-services-inc/
    - Fact: RFPs from sophisticated clients are now sophisticated enough to score AI governance
      as part of bid evaluation. The advice circulating in the market is that a specific,
      one-page AI policy attached to a proposal reads as competence, while a generic disclaimer
      reads as a firm that has not thought about it.
    - Why it matters: This is the demand-side proof that "lagging behind" is not hypothetical.
      The market is not waiting for firms to decide whether AI is a risk or an opportunity. It
      is already grading firms on whether they have a governed answer to the question at all,
      which reframes the reader's choice from "adopt or not" to "have a defensible position or
      not."
    - Uncertain: No public RFP-scoring rubric was found and cited directly; this is reported as
      market practice by one advisory source and should be framed as an emerging signal, not an
      established standard.

## Pattern across the stories

Two threads run through nearly every entry and they point at each other. First: the mechanism
by which a firm "gives away" its IP is rarely a dramatic leak. It is the accumulation of
default settings, consumer-tool habits, and vendor contract language nobody renegotiated
(Autodesk's ToU, Cyberhaven's paste data, the AIA and EJCDC forms still silent on AI). Second:
the firms treating this seriously are not choosing between adoption and abstention. They are
choosing what they own versus what they rent, deliberately: AECOM bought the model, some AEC
firms are negotiating training-restriction clauses into their own engagement letters, some
professional-services firms are turning zero-retention into a sales line. The AI labs
complicate the picture further by moving into the consulting market themselves while
partly bankrolling the largest incumbents, which means the "differentiate or get disrupted"
question is not only about a firm's own AI use. It is about who else is now capitalized to sell
the same judgment the firm sells. The honest middle ground the user is asking about is visible
in the data: most firms are already exposed to the risk (53 to 88% use some AI) without having
captured the offsetting value (63% stuck in pilots, only 18% tracking ROI, 6% capturing
enterprise value), which suggests the real failure mode is not choosing a side. It is drifting
into exposure without ever making the choice explicit.

## Addendum: the technical mechanism (added after angle revision)

The first draft from this digest was rejected as generic and too business/contract focused. The
revision needs the actual technical mechanics of what "training on your data" means versus
"using your data," since that distinction is where the real, ownable insight sits and it is
largely absent from the entries above. Direct competitor AECOM must not be named or used as a
case study in the piece going forward.

13. **Models memorize and can regurgitate training data verbatim, and it scales with repetition**
    - Source: Carlini et al., "Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models," ICLR
      2023 | arXiv:2202.07646
    - Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646
    - Fact: The paper shows GPT-J memorized at least 1% of its training data by the authors'
      extractability definition, meaning that content can be reproduced verbatim given the right
      prompt. Memorization follows a near log-linear relationship with three factors: model
      capacity (a tenfold increase in parameters raised memorization by about 19 percentage
      points in their tests), how many times a given string is duplicated in the training set,
      and how much context is used to prompt for it. This built on Carlini's earlier "Extracting
      Training Data from Large Language Models" (USENIX Security 2021), the first paper to
      demonstrate the attack.
    - Why it matters: This is the mechanism, not the anecdote. Training does not politely
      summarize what it read. It can store and later emit exact fragments. Critically, the
      biggest driver of extractability after model size is duplication: a string that appears
      many times in training data is far more memorizable than one that appears once. For an
      engineering firm this reframes the risk. A firm's most valuable, differentiated judgment on
      a genuinely novel problem is comparatively rare in any training corpus, and rarity resists
      memorization. Boilerplate specification language, standard notes, and calculation templates
      that many engineers at many firms paste into the same consumer AI tool are duplicated
      constantly across the industry, which is exactly the condition the research shows makes
      content extractable. The mundane material is the most exposed material, not the clever
      material.
    - Uncertain: This is lab research on open, smaller models (GPT-J, GPT-2 class) studied because
      their training data is public and inspectable. Frontier closed models (GPT-5 class, Claude,
      Gemini) are not directly auditable this way, so the exact memorization rate for the tools a
      firm actually uses is not independently verifiable. The mechanism is well established. The
      current-model magnitude is not.

14. **"Reads" and "trains" are different technical events, and vendor contracts increasingly say
    which one applies**
    - Source: OpenAI enterprise privacy documentation (openai.com/enterprise-privacy), corroborated
      via secondary comparison at protecto.ai; Anthropic's published consumer terms update
    - Links: https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/ ;
      https://www.protecto.ai/blog/openai-data-privacy/ ;
      https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms
    - Fact: OpenAI's API and Enterprise tier is not used to train models by default, and a
      fine-tuned model built on that tier is isolated to the customer that built it, never served
      to other customers or folded into a future base model. Anthropic's Claude for Work, Claude
      Gov, and API deployments carry the same exclusion. Both companies' consumer tiers (plain
      ChatGPT, plain Claude.ai) work the other way: consumer chats can be used to train future
      models unless the user opts out, and Anthropic's opt-in retention window runs up to five
      years.
    - Why it matters: The tier a firm's staff actually use, not the vendor's brand name, decides
      whether "using AI" means reading a file once or feeding it into the next model version. An
      engineer on a personal ChatGPT account and an engineer on the firm's enterprise seat are
      using functionally different products with respect to IP, even though the interface looks
      identical.
    - Uncertain: These are contractual and policy commitments, not something a firm can verify by
      inspection. "Not used for training" is enforced by the vendor's internal process, not by
      any external audit the firm can run itself.

15. **RAG keeps data in a store the firm controls; fine-tuning bakes it into weights**
    - Source: IBM, "RAG vs. Fine-tuning"; Databricks, "RAG vs Fine Tuning: Enterprise Decisions"
      | both 2026
    - Links: https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/rag-vs-fine-tuning ;
      https://www.databricks.com/blog/rag-vs-fine-tuning
    - Fact: Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) does not change a model's weights at all. It
      keeps a firm's documents in a separate, access-controlled index and pulls the relevant
      passage into the prompt at the moment of the question, so the underlying model never learns
      the content, it only reads it on demand. Fine-tuning does the opposite: it updates the
      model's weights using the firm's data, which is what creates the memorization exposure
      described in the Carlini research above. Enterprise AI teams lean toward RAG for this and
      other reasons; over 70% reportedly use RAG as their primary technique versus fewer than 25%
      relying on standalone fine-tuning, per industry surveys cited by both sources.
    - Why it matters: This is the concrete, ownable technical distinction the piece can teach a
      partner who is not an engineer of models: asking a vendor "do you fine-tune on our data" is
      a different and more important question than asking "do you use AI." The answer determines
      whether the firm's judgment gets stored in a machine's memory or merely consulted by one.
    - Uncertain: The 70% and 25% adoption figures are vendor-cited industry survey numbers rather
      than one traceable primary study, and should be attributed loosely rather than as precise
      fact.

16. **Self-hosted open-weight models remove the transmission question entirely**
    - Source: Industry deployment guides aggregating 2026 open-weight releases (Llama 4, DeepSeek
      V4, Qwen 3.5, Gemma 4) and the vLLM inference project
    - Link: https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/self-hosting-open-weight-llms-2026-deployment-decision-guide
    - Fact: A firm can run an open-weight model (Meta's Llama 4, DeepSeek's V4, Alibaba's Qwen
      3.5, Google's Gemma 4) on its own infrastructure, paired with a self-hosted vector database
      for retrieval, using the open-source vLLM inference engine. In this architecture, project
      data never leaves the firm's own servers, so there is no vendor training policy to trust in
      the first place.
    - Why it matters: This is the maximal version of the RAG answer, for firms handling the most
      sensitive project data. The trade-off is real: open-weight models generally trail the
      frontier proprietary models (GPT and Claude's latest) on the hardest reasoning tasks, so a
      firm buys certainty about its data at some cost in capability.
    - Uncertain: The claim that "over half the LLM market runs on-premises" comes from a single
      vendor-adjacent deployment guide, not an independently verifiable market survey, and should
      not be quoted as a hard statistic. The capability gap between open-weight and frontier
      closed models is narrowing but real and changes month to month, so any specific comparison
      should be dated and hedged.
